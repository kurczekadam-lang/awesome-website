It's Not About the Picture; It's About the Message :  How IKEA's Pictures Show More Than a Sofa

A few brands are as recognizable as IKEA, and little of that recognition comes from the products. A bookshelf is not at all memorable, unless it's a magic bookshelf your grandpa gave you on your 15th birthday. What makes that bookshelf memorable is the image of a lively living room, where the bookshelf holds child photos, your half-read novel, Wish it. Want it. Do it, and a plant you swear never dies. IKEA's photography captures the brand's identity in the images you see while scrolling, in a magazine, or on IKEA's website. I hope to show how it works and how effective it is.

Perception Vs. Image

Brand identity is two things, what a company wants to be perceived as vs. how consumers see the brand [1]. Photography is in between that, since they demonstrate what the brand means in people's minds. Like they say, a picture is worth a thousand words. The question is weather IKEA's phography and their customers' perception are at the same level.

Images are like Books; They can Be Read

De La Ville and Krupicka [2] studied IKEA's catalogue and advertising images to find the semiotics within the advertising. It separates the picture from what it shows and the idea it carries.


For example, this picture shows a sofa, a rug, and a coffee table. The idea, the laptop on the table, the thrown blankets, and the sunk in pillows are all signs that this scene has been lived in.

This one is a little tricky since there Is a lot going on. This picture shows people eating and talking over a kitchen table. The kitchen table is secondary idea in this image, rather the primary is the feeling of comfort, and togetherness.

This picture shows off a clothing rack being used within a confined space. The idea is the problem the clothing rack solves whilst in a confined space. Zoom in on the bottom of the clothing rack; enhance. Those are sandals not being in the way; those sandals are neatly tucked away with your clothes rather than clothes and sandals being everywhere.

Genius, right? IKEA's  images allow you to set foot in the scene. This is also great for provoking the viewers' imagination, giving them ideas to apply to their lives. Small details make the biggest difference.

Did You Know IKEA is Very Swedish? Shocker.

Moran argues that for over 70 years the IKEA catalog has been reinforcing and spreading a Swedish image [3]. Aligning itself with Scandinavian Modernism. I don't think this was on purpose but rather a by-product of imagery. The light wood, bright rooms, and minimal style carry value, value that says good design is for everyone. According to IKEA [4], the catalogs did very well, at most 220 million printed, in 69 versions, in 32 languages. The former head of communications of IKEA said: Historically speaking, the catalogue has by far been the most important tool for positioning IKEA as a brand.

The IKEA Effect

The IKEA effect is people placing higher value on products when they assemble them themselves, but only when they can usefully build the product [5]. What the heck does that have to do with photography? Hold your horses, pal. A finished personalized room makes the products look achievable to finish and creates a desire to purchase. The photos are the outcome, whilst the customer is expected to do the work.

How Can Apply IKEA's Photography Strategy?

Make sure you Do Not apply this one-for-one; adapt to your environment and background. Use what seems useful and forget the rest. I don't think you are selling Swedish designed furniture, so like I said, apply what you want.

  1. Make it authentic. If your values are honesty and you want an interpersonal connection with your customers, they need to feel real to them. Make the scene feel lived in and real. Before you shoot, ask yourself, can my viewer see themselves there?
  2. Make it timeless. IKEA doesn't abide to trends that will die out; they go for what will last.  Master your lighting, capture the mood, and keep it simple.  Ask yourself, will I use this picture in 5 years?
  3. Have a goal. If you don't know why you are taking a photo, then why will your viewer care? Know where this photo will be showcased, what you are trying to communicate, what you are physically showing, and what the idea behind it is.

Super Short Conclusion

There is a lot to photography, and IKEA takes full advantage of that. People want to buy IKEA's products because of what they imagine around them. Be like IKEA, maybe not so Swedish, unless you want to be.
